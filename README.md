What Exactly Does a Pulmonologist Do?
A pulmonologist is a physician who specializes in diagnosing and treating diseases that affect the lungs and respiratory system. While a general physician handles common infections, a pulmonologist goes deeper — managing complex, chronic, and life-threatening conditions involving the airways, lung tissue, pulmonary circulation, and breathing mechanics.
Think of your lungs as a sophisticated air-exchange system working 20,000 times a day without you asking. A pulmonologist knows every layer of that system — and when something disrupts it, whether it's inflammation, infection, or structural damage, they're the specialist you need.

6 Signs You Shouldn't Ignore
1. A Cough Lasting More Than 3 Weeks Medically considered "chronic," a persistent cough can be the earliest sign of asthma, tuberculosis, or even early-stage lung cancer — not just allergies.
2. Unexplained Breathlessness Getting winded during activities that used to feel effortless isn't just "being out of shape." By the time you consciously notice breathlessness, lung function may have already declined significantly.
3. Wheezing or Whistling When You Breathe This signals narrowed or inflamed airways — commonly associated with asthma, but also COPD or heart conditions affecting the lungs.
4. Recurring Chest Infections Catching bronchitis or pneumonia more than once a year points to an underlying pattern worth investigating with a specialist.
5. Coughing Up Blood Never wait on this. Even a small amount of blood warrants immediate evaluation to rule out TB, lung cancer, or pulmonary embolism.
6. Loud Snoring and Constant Daytime Fatigue Obstructive sleep apnea is dramatically underdiagnosed. If you wake up exhausted despite a full night's sleep, a pulmonologist can conduct a sleep study and design targeted treatment.
What Happens at Your First Pulmonology Appointment
Your first visit typically involves a detailed history — your symptoms, smoking history, occupational exposures, and family history of lung disease. From there, your pulmonologist will likely order a spirometry test, which measures how much air you can move and how quickly — the backbone of diagnosing conditions like asthma and COPD.
Additional tests may include chest X-ray or CT scan, arterial blood gas analysis, bronchoscopy, or a sleep study, depending on your presentation.
What separates a good pulmonologist from a great one isn't just technical skill — it's their ability to connect test results to your unique story, explain findings in plain language, and design a plan you can actually follow.
